Project Description

This package documents the structural design and post-incident review of three residential shelters constructed within the Meadow Plains district during the summer and autumn of 1951. All three structures were designed independently by their respective occupants (Pig One, Pig Two, Pig Three) without formal engineering oversight. This office was retained following the wind-loading event of October 3, 1951 to conduct structural assessment and issue recommendations for minimum construction standards in the district.

Engineer's Opinion

It is the professional opinion of this office that Straw and Stick construction is wholly inadequate for residential use in any district with known large-carnivore respiratory hazard classifications. Neither Unit 1 nor Unit 2 met minimum structural requirements for the applied load conditions, and their failure was entirely predictable from first principles.

Unit 3, constructed to a substantially higher specification by Pig Three, performed exactly as a properly engineered masonry structure should. Its survival under the October 3 event is unremarkable from an engineering standpoint — it was built correctly.

Brick construction per Sheet 4 specifications is hereby recommended as the minimum acceptable standard for all future residential construction in the Meadow Plains district and surrounding areas within the known wolf respiratory range.

APPROVED — A. Stone P.E.

Formal Recommendations
#RECOMMENDATION
R-01All new residential construction shall use minimum Grade A fired brick or equivalent engineered masonry.
R-02Reinforced concrete pad foundations (min. 18 in. depth) required in all large-carnivore respiratory hazard zones.
R-03Full perimeter shear wall required. No exemptions for single-occupancy dwellings.
R-04All structures must be rated for minimum 80 mph wind uplift. Wolf respiratory events shall be classified as a design load condition.
R-05Straw and ungraded timber prohibited as primary structural materials in this jurisdiction effective immediately.
Lessons Learned

The Meadow Plains wind-loading event of October 3, 1951 provides a clear and unambiguous case study in the consequences of under-engineered residential construction. Three structures were subjected to identical load conditions. Two failed. One did not. The outcome was entirely determined by the quality of construction prior to the event — not by luck, not by circumstance.

The occupant of Unit 3 (Pig Three) made a rational engineering decision: use the best available material, build a proper foundation, and do not cut corners. That decision, made weeks before the threat materialized, is the reason all three pigs are alive today.

The occupants of Units 1 and 2 made different decisions — prioritizing speed and convenience over structural integrity. Those decisions were corrected only by the fortunate circumstance that Unit 3 was available as a refuge. In another scenario, that refuge would not have existed.

FINAL NOTE — A. STONE P.E. All three pigs are safe. The brick house stands. This report and all supporting documentation are hereby submitted as evidence in Case No. 51-CRIM-0847 (The District v. B. Wolf, Re: Destruction of Property × 2, Criminal Huffing with Intent, Menacing). The structural evidence is unambiguous.

The wolf blew. The bricks held. Case closed.
